Now the material itself. Brutalist buildings can use concrete, brick, glass, steel, timber, rough-hewn stone, even gabions — but raw concrete dominates, and the reason is prosaic: it is cheap. It is then left to reveal the basic nature of its construction, which is why the surface is rough and why the wood shuttering pattern is still legible in the wall. That pattern is the imprint of the boards used to build the forms the concrete was cast into, on site. Nobody polished it off, because polishing it off would be the dishonest move. Two other habits follow from the same logic. Buildings are made from recurring modular elements that represent specific functional zones, distinctly articulated and grouped into a unified whole; and there is often an emphasis on graphic expression, in the elevations and in the whole-site plan, in regard to the main functions and people-flows of the building. The results are frequently massive in character even when they are not large, and they challenge traditional notions of what a building should look like, with as much attention given to interior space as to exterior.
