This is the page where the movement defines itself, and it does so as a moral position rather than a look. New brutalism was a philosophical approach to design — a striving for simple, honest, functional buildings that accommodate their purpose, inhabitants and location, and a strict modernist language set against the retrospective nostalgia of much nineteen forties architecture. Peter Smithson put the heart of it in two sentences: brutalism is not concerned with the material as such but rather the quality of material, and the seeing of materials for what they were — the woodness of the wood, the sandiness of sand. John Voelcker agreed that the movement could not be understood through stylistic analysis at all, supporting the Smithsons' own description of it as an ethic, not an aesthetic. Banham then did something useful with that: he turned the ethic into three testable conditions. Formal legibility of plan. Clear exhibition of structure. Valuation of materials for their inherent qualities, as found. He added a fourth consideration, the aesthetic image — the coherence of the building as a visual entity. Keep those three numbered terms; we come back to them at the end.
