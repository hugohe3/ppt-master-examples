From one Swedish house the term travelled fast. In the summer of nineteen fifty a group of visiting English architects, including Michael Ventris, Oliver Cox and Graeme Shankland, picked it up, and it spread, in the phrase recorded at the time, like wildfire among a certain faction of young British architects. Three years later Alison Smithson put new brutalism into print for the first time, describing an unbuilt house in Soho in the November issue of Architectural Design, with the intention she stated plainly in the box on the right: to have the structure exposed entirely, without interior finishes wherever practicable. In nineteen fifty-four Hunstanton School completed in Norfolk — the first finished building anywhere that its own architects called new brutalist, and described at the time as the most truly modern building in England. The Sugden House followed in Watford a year later. Hunstanton itself was likely inspired by Mies van der Rohe's nineteen forty-six Alumni Memorial Hall in Chicago, so even the first brutalist building had a modernist parent.
