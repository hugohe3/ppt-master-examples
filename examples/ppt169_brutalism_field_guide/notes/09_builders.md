This table is the answer to the assumption that brutalism was a British problem. Within about two decades the same language of concrete was being spoken in at least eight countries, and the source names the architects. In Britain the pioneers are Alison and Peter Smithson, with Ernő Goldfinger, Chamberlin, Powell and Bon at the Barbican, Denys Lasdun at the National Theatre, Norman Engleback at the Hayward Gallery, and the London County Council architects' department behind a great deal of the rest. In the United States Evans Woollen the third brought the style to Indianapolis, Walter Netsch built academic buildings with it, and Marcel Breuer softened it with curves instead of corners — while Louis Kahn, who was opposed to what he saw as the muscular posturing of most brutalism, was nonetheless informed by some of the same ideas. Australia and Canada each produced whole institutional programmes; Clorindo Testa carried it in Argentina, Mihajlo Mitrović in Serbia, Garol Isakovich in Vietnam during the subsidy era, and Slobodan Jovandić in Bosnia and Herzegovina. The point of the grid is the spread, not the individual row.
