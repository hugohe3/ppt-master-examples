The movement itself was largely over by the late nineteen seventies and early eighties, having given way to structural expressionism and deconstructivism. What brought it back was print. From twenty fifteen a run of guides and books appeared — Brutal London, the Brutalist London Map, This Brutal World, SOS Brutalism, and the Atlas of Brutalist Architecture — and interest in the style resurged with them. New construction followed, under the name neobrutalism. Two of its best-known examples are attached to Pritzker citations. Wang Shu's Ningbo Museum of two thousand and eight, shown here, keeps the traces of its bamboo framework visible on the monumental concrete, and was called an urban icon in his twenty twelve citation. The campus of the private University of Engineering and Technology in Peru, by Yvonne Farrell and Shelley McNamara, appears in their twenty twenty citation. Notice the reversal, though: neobrutalist buildings tend to be commissioned by the private sector rather than the state, and they may use recycled brick or timber rather than concrete, while still exposing their materials and structure. Many of the defining aspects have been softened too — façades sandblasted to a stone-like surface, covered in stucco, or made of patterned precast elements, as in the redevelopment of Sheffield's Park Hill. But genuine board-marked concrete is still being poured, as at the Silberrad Student Centre at the University of Essex in twenty fifteen.
