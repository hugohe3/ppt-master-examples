The largest single patron of brutalism was not the housing authority. It was the university. In Britain the earliest examples are the beehives at St John's College, Oxford, and the nineteen fifty-nine architecture department extension at Cambridge under Leslie Martin, designed by Colin St John Wilson and Alex Hardy with the students taking part, which then inspired the listed University Centre. James Stirling's Red Trilogy runs through the same decade: the Leicester Engineering Building with James Gowan, sometimes called Britain's first postmodern building; the History Faculty at Cambridge; and the Florey Building at Oxford. Then the new universities gave brutalist architects whole sites. Basil Spence's Falmer House at Sussex is listed Grade One and was described as a meeting of Arts and Crafts with modernism, with hand-made bricks and colonnades of bare board-marked concrete arches inspired by the Colosseum. Denys Lasdun's ziggurats at East Anglia — six linked halls in Norfolk Terrace, four in Suffolk Terrace, with the library and teaching wall between them — are considered one of the finest brutalist campuses of the decade. At Durham, Ove Arup's Kingsgate Bridge is one of only six post-nineteen sixty-one buildings listed Grade One by twenty seventeen, and Dunelm House was saved from demolition in twenty twenty-one after a five-year campaign. America has Paul Rudolph at Yale, where as department chair he was both client and architect, and William Pereira's Geisel Library, which was meant to be steel and glass until cost pushed the structure outside in concrete. And then the black band: the ziggurats closed in twenty twenty-three during the reinforced autoclaved aerated concrete crisis, with no refurbishment date set as of February twenty twenty-five. Even the finest examples are not safe from their own material.
