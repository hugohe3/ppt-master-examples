# 02_what_it_counts

The measure in front of you is narrower and stranger than most people expect. Chronic absenteeism means missing ten percent or more of school days for any reason at all, which in a one hundred and eighty day year is eighteen days. Excused illness counts. Unexcused days count. Days out on suspension count. That is why a student can be chronically absent and never once be recorded as truant, and it is why this is an operating metric rather than a discipline problem. There is a second counting rule worth knowing: the federal report card measure is fifteen days or more, and the Every Student Succeeds Act requires states to publish the rate. The definition and both counting rules come from the Framework for Action published through ERIC. The Riverbend baseline of thirty one percent, and the three thousand one hundred students behind it, are illustrative figures for this example.

# 03_citywide_trend

The takeaway on this page is that the attendance crisis did not close when buildings reopened. This is New York City, the largest district that publishes this series, used here as a benchmark and not as Riverbend's own numbers. The city ran at twenty six point four percent chronically absent in twenty eighteen nineteen, peaked at forty point two percent in twenty twenty one twenty two, and has come back only to thirty six point three percent. That recovery closes less than half the gap, four years after the disruption began. The method matters for how much weight you put on it. This is a student weighted rate, computed by summing chronically absent students and students contributing ten or more total days across every school row for each year, not by averaging school percentages. The data is the New York City Open Data end of year attendance dataset.

# 04_district_spread

The second thing the published data shows is that this problem is not evenly spread, which changes what a plan should target. In one city, in one school year, the thirty two geographic community school districts ran from fifteen point five percent in District twenty to forty six point two percent in District twenty three. The median district sat at twenty eight point nine percent. Eight of the thirty two were at or above thirty five percent while six were at or below twenty percent. A district wide average sitting in the middle of that range is an accounting figure; it describes almost none of the schools inside it. We have excluded District seventy five, the citywide special education district, at fifty one point five percent, because its population is not comparable to a geographic district. Same source, the by district dataset.

# 05_grade_curve

If the plan is going to be aimed somewhere, this page says where. Across the K to twelve span in twenty twenty two twenty three, the rate is worst at both ends: forty five point six percent in kindergarten and forty five point two percent in grade twelve, with the trough at grade five still near twenty nine percent. The kindergarten peak is the one that usually surprises a board, and it is the one with the longest consequences. The Framework for Action reports that students chronically absent in both kindergarten and first grade are much less likely to read at grade level by third grade, and that students not reading at grade level by third grade are four times more likely to drop out. By sixth grade chronic absenteeism is itself a leading dropout indicator, and by high school attendance predicts dropout better than test scores do. So a system that only watches high school is watching one end of a two ended problem.

# 06_four_causes

Before the recommendation, one page on cause, because it determines what kind of response works. Researchers group the reasons students miss school into four kinds. Barriers are conditions the family cannot simply decide their way past: unmanaged asthma, diabetes, mental health and vision problems, transport and housing instability. The Environmental Protection Agency estimate cited in the same report puts ten and a half million school days lost to asthma alone each year. Aversions are reasons to stay away: bullying, undiagnosed disabilities, overly punitive discipline. Disengagement is a negative school climate or the absence of one supportive relationship. Myths are the beliefs that keep families comfortable with absence, including the idea that an excused day does not count. Only one of those four is anything like refusal, and none of them is solved by a letter home. The illustration beside it is a drawing, not a photograph of any school.

# 07_recommendation

Here is what we are proposing. Build one attendance system with three tiers and staff it inside the district. Tier one is universal prevention for every student. Tier two is early intervention for the students who are slipping toward the ten percent line. Tier three is coordinated case support for the students already in crisis. This is not our invention; the Framework for Action identifies a comprehensive multi tiered system of supports, combining prevention with early and consistent interventions, as the most effective response. The division of labour we are proposing is that Lantern designs the system and trains the site leads, and Riverbend runs it. The staffing behind that, one attendance lead per site and one district coordinator, is an illustrative figure at this stage.

---

# 08_tier_detail

The tiers only mean something if each one has a trigger, an owner and a limit, so here they are. Tier one triggers on enrolment: attendance expectations, positive messaging and daily attendance data in front of every teacher, owned by the site principal. Tier two triggers when a student passes the early warning threshold in a rolling window: same week family contact, a named adult, and a barrier check covering health and transport, owned by the site attendance lead. Tier three triggers when a student is already chronically absent or is referred by the team: case review, a home visit, a community partner referral and an attendance plan agreed with the family, owned by the district attendance coordinator. The thresholds and role titles here are illustrative. The limit is the part I want to be explicit about. The Framework treats legal action as a very last resort, and England's statutory guidance takes the same shape, with legal intervention reserved for cases where attendance support does not work, is not engaged with, or is not appropriate. This system escalates on data, not on frustration.

# 09_year_one

Year one is ten months and four phases, and every phase ends in something you can inspect. Phase one, September to November, is baseline and root cause: an attendance data audit, a barrier survey and site interviews, delivered as a baseline report with a root cause profile for each site. Phase two, December to February, designs and builds the early warning thresholds, the teacher dashboard specification and the family contact playbook, delivered as the attendance operating manual. Phase three, March to May, runs tier one and tier two live at three schools with weekly case review, delivered as pilot results measured against the baseline. Phase four, June to August, trains the attendance lead at every site. What we need from the district is on the same page: data access within two weeks of start, a named executive sponsor, released time for the site leads, and a family contact consent position under district policy. Every date and deliverable on this page is illustrative.

# 13_projected_effect

I want to be careful with this page, because it is the one most likely to be quoted back without its qualifiers. This is a model, not a measurement. It starts from the illustrative thirty one percent baseline and shows a modeled contribution from each tier, two and a half points from universal prevention, four points from early intervention and two and a half points from intensive support, reaching a modeled twenty two percent by June twenty twenty eight. None of it is derived from the New York City series we showed earlier. It rests on four assumptions that are worth testing: data access from month one, one trained attendance lead released at every site, family contact in the same week of a tier two trigger, and no change to the calendar or bell schedule. It fails first at staffing, and the response to that is the phase gate itself, which holds rollout until leads are named. If data access slips, the baseline narrows to the four largest sites. If partner capacity is short, tier three stays at the pilot sites while agreements catch up.
