## III. Visual Theme

### Theme Style

- **Mode**: custom
- **Mode References**: pyramid
- **Mode Behavior**: Run the proposal in the installed Style's order rather than a pure conclusion-first stack: one page of the client's situation in their own terms, an evidence arc that establishes the problem is structural and unevenly distributed, then the recommendation stated as a single dominant commitment, and after it only material that makes that commitment credible — mechanism, sequence, scope boundary, price, projected effect and its assumptions — closing on one action with an owner and a date. Every page title states what the district gets or learns, never a capability name; each page carries exactly one commitment, one component of the approach, or one piece of proof; and every claim is visibly typed as measured evidence, proposed commitment, or illustrative assumption.
- **Visual style**: custom
- **Visual Style References**: soft-rounded, editorial
- **Visual Style Behavior**: soft-rounded owns the deck's containers and rhythm — 16 px radii, low soft elevation, generous gutters, a warm paper field behind white cards — so a two-year ask reads as approachable rather than austere. editorial owns the evidence pages: hairline rules under titles and above source lines, a strict left-aligned column grid, small-caps eyebrow labels, and a visible source line under every chart and table. Density is deliberately unequal: argument and decision pages breathe, while scope, pricing, and traceability pages carry real detail under one grid without dropping below legible scale. Decoration stays professional and calm — no gradient banners, no heavy corporate ornament, nothing that looks more expensive than the work being proposed.
- **Theme**: A district's school day rebuilt as a system that notices. The recurring motif is the lantern arc — a single thin amber arc that appears at the corner of anchor pages and becomes the connective line of the tier diagram and the roadmap, carrying the identity of Lantern Education Partners without a logo wall.
- **Tone**: Direct, respectful of the board's time, unsentimental about the evidence, and explicit about what is not yet proven.

### AI Image Strategy

- **Image Rendering**: custom
- **Image Rendering References**: vector-illustration, editorial
- **Image Rendering Behavior**: Restrained editorial vector illustration — clean flat shapes with a single weight of confident outline, no gradients and no photographic texture, built from the deck's own anchors (deep teal-slate structure, warm paper ground, one lantern-amber light source per image) with quiet mid-tone shading for depth. Subjects are the ordinary objects of a school morning seen from a middle distance; figures are absent or reduced to distant silhouettes, and no lettering, signage, numeral, or interface text appears anywhere in the artwork.
- **Visual**: Flat vector scenes of school-day objects and thresholds — a classroom at first light, a front door and a waiting bus at the edge of a neighborhood morning, an open school entrance — drawn in the deck's palette with one warm light source.
- **Mood**: Calm, early, unresolved but not bleak — the visual register of a well-made public-service report cover rather than a stock education photograph.

## V. Layout Principles

### Deck-wide Direction

- **Hierarchy direction**: Attention enters at the page title, which always states the takeaway, drops to one dominant object (a chart, a diagram, a table, or a single commitment block), and exits at the source line or the labeled boundary at the foot of the page.
- **Composition tendency**: Keep one stable position for the commitment line and one stable position for supporting evidence so the deck navigates by position when it is read offline; let the recommendation, commercial, and next-step pages hold open space, and let architecture, scope, and pricing pages carry structured density under the same grid.
- **Cross-page continuity**: The lantern arc recurs at the corner of anchor pages and becomes the connective geometry of the tier diagram and the roadmap; the tier color ramp (light teal → teal → deep slate) means the same thing on every page it appears; every evidence page ends with a source line in the same position; every page carrying invented figures shows the same illustrative label in the same corner.
- **Spacing posture**: Variable by page rhythm — anchor and breathing pages open, evidence and scope pages dense within one grid.
- **Spacing anchors**: page margin 64, block gap 32, column gutter 32, corner radius 16, body leading 34.

## IX. Content Outline

### Part 1: The situation

#### Slide 01 - Cover

- **Audience move**: Board members arrive expecting another vendor pitch → they see the subject named in the district's own terms and know exactly what is real and what is illustrative in this document.
- **Relationships**: The deck title, the two-year commitment, the proposing party, and the illustrative-example disclosure are one identity block; the disclosure qualifies every other unit on the page (parent).
- **Composition**: One focal claim on a warm solid field, with the classroom image held inside a rounded frame in the lower or right zone; solid-color type only, never set over the image.
- **Title**: Every Day Counts
- **Core message**: A two-year plan to cut chronic absenteeism at Riverbend Unified, built on measured evidence and priced as illustrative.
- **Content**: Deck title "Every Day Counts" · subtitle "A two-year plan to reduce chronic absenteeism at Riverbend Unified" · Prepared by Lantern Education Partners for the Riverbend Unified Board of Education, September 2026 · illustrative-example disclosure: Lantern Education Partners and Riverbend Unified are fictional, and every price, headcount, date, and projected effect in this deck is illustrative; the problem evidence is real and sourced on the page where it appears
- **Cover impact**: The hook is the phrase every board already uses about attendance, turned into the document's promise — and immediately under it, the unusual honesty of a proposal that labels its own numbers as illustrative.
- **Images**: cover_classroom_first_light.jpg as a contained frame; the title never sits over it.
- **Motion suggestion**: The title block settles first and the disclosure line arrives after it, so the audience reads the promise and then the boundary; the image frame holds still.
- **Data class: scenario** — the client name, the vendor name, and the September 2026 date are illustrative.

#### Slide 02 - What "chronically absent" actually counts

- **Audience move**: Believes absence means truancy and a discipline problem → understands the measure counts excused, unexcused, and suspension days alike, and that Riverbend's own number is an operating figure, not a judgment.
- **Relationships**: The federal definition and the report-card definition are two ways of counting the same condition (link); excused, unexcused, and suspended days are members of one count (membership); the illustrative district baseline is an instance of that definition applied locally (parent).
- **Composition**: Dominant hero number with the definition beside it, and the three absence types as one banded row underneath.
- **Title**: A student can be chronically absent without ever being truant
- **Core message**: Chronic absenteeism is a measured condition — missing a tenth of the school year for any reason — which is why it can be managed like any other operating metric.
- **Content**: Hero number 18 days — 10% of a 180-day year (arithmetic shown) · definition block: chronic absenteeism is most commonly defined as missing 10 percent or more of school days for any reason — excused, unexcused, or suspension (Framework for Action, ERIC ED617466, 2017) · second counting rule: the U.S. Department of Education's report-card measure is 15 days or more in a school year, and ESSA requires the rate on state and local report cards (same source) · three-part band: excused · unexcused · suspension, all counted · illustrative Riverbend baseline: 31% of students chronically absent in 2025-26, about 3,100 of 10,000 enrolled, labeled illustrative · closing line: the measure is already collected, so nothing new has to be invented to manage it
- **Mathematical content**: 0.10 \times 180 = 18
- **Data class: scenario** — the 31% baseline, the 3,100 students, and the 10,000 enrollment are illustrative; the definitions and the ESSA reporting requirement are sourced.

#### Slide 03 - The problem did not end when school reopened

- **Audience move**: Assumes the attendance crisis was a pandemic episode now closing → sees a real five-year benchmark series in which the rate is still far above where it started.
- **Relationships**: Five school years form one ordered series (order); the 2018-19 rate and the 2022-23 rate stand in direct contrast; the 2021-22 peak and the partial recovery are linked points in that series.
- **Composition**: Wide chart dominant with the takeaway stated above it and the source line beneath.
- **Title**: Four years later, the rate is still ten points above where it started
- **Core message**: In the largest district with published data, chronic absenteeism rose by half and has only partly come back — this is a structural condition, not a one-year shock.
- **Content**: Chart of the citywide rate by school year: 2018-19 26.4% · 2019-20 24.9% · 2020-21 29.2% · 2021-22 40.2% · 2022-23 36.3% · takeaway: the peak was 2021-22 and the recovery since has closed less than half the gap · grouping statement: computed from the NYC DOE end-of-year attendance file by summing chronically absent students and students contributing 10+ total days across all "All Grades" school rows in each year, then dividing — a student-weighted citywide rate, not an average of school rates · benchmark caution: New York City is used as a published reference series, not as Riverbend's own numbers · source line: NYC Open Data, End-of-Year Attendance and Chronic Absenteeism (sgsi-66kk), 2018-19 to 2022-23
- **Visualization**: citywide-trend — a five-point series of the citywide chronic-absenteeism rate by school year, percent labels on every point, explicit axis titles for school year and percent of students chronically absent. Native-ready: citywide-trend=yes
- **Motion suggestion**: The series builds left to right so the 2021-22 peak lands before the partial recovery; the axis frame is present from the start.

#### Slide 04 - The average hides the schools that need help

- **Audience move**: Thinks a district-wide rate is the number to manage → sees a threefold spread inside one city in one year and accepts that resources must follow the distribution, not the mean.
- **Relationships**: Thirty-two districts form one ranked distribution (order); the lowest and highest districts stand in contrast; the median marks the midpoint of the same set (membership).
- **Composition**: Full-width sorted distribution with only the extremes and the midpoint annotated; everything else stays unlabeled.
- **Title**: In the same city, in the same year, district rates ran from 16% to 46%
- **Core message**: Chronic absenteeism concentrates — a district average is an accounting figure, and the plan has to be aimed at the tail.
- **Content**: Chart of all 32 geographic community school districts, 2018-19, sorted low to high · annotated points: lowest 15.5% (District 20), median 28.9%, highest 46.2% (District 23) · eight of the 32 districts sat at or above 35% while six sat at or below 20% · exclusion note: District 75, the citywide special-education district, is excluded from this comparison at 51.5% because its population is not comparable to a geographic district · grouping statement: the published "All Grades / All Students" rate for each district in 2018-19, taken as reported · benchmark caution repeated: reference series, not Riverbend's numbers · source line: NYC Open Data, End-of-Year Attendance and Chronic Absenteeism by District (hags-jh3e), 2016-17 to 2020-21
- **Visualization**: district-spread — 32 sorted category columns of percent chronically absent with the lowest, median, and highest annotated, explicit axis titles for community school district and percent of students chronically absent. Native-ready: district-spread=yes

#### Slide 05 - It starts in kindergarten and ends in senior year

- **Audience move**: Assumes the problem belongs to high school → sees both ends of the K-12 span carrying the highest rates and understands why the plan starts in the early grades as well as the last one.
- **Relationships**: Thirteen grade levels form one ordered span (order); the kindergarten and grade-12 rates stand together against the grade-5 trough (contrast); early-grade absence and later dropout risk are linked by the framework's evidence (link).
- **Composition**: Wide curve dominant, with the two peaks called out in the takeaway and the framework's consequence stated as a short block beside or beneath the chart.
- **Title**: The curve has two peaks, and one of them is five-year-olds
- **Core message**: Absence is worst at the entry to school and at the exit from it, so an attendance system that only watches high school is watching one end of the problem.
- **Content**: Chart of the 2022-23 citywide rate by grade: K 45.6% · 1 38.7% · 2 34.7% · 3 31.7% · 4 30.3% · 5 29.1% · 6 29.4% · 7 31.5% · 8 33.5% · 9 34.9% · 10 35.8% · 11 34.4% · 12 45.2% · takeaway: the lowest grade in the span still sits near 29% · why the early peak matters: students chronically absent in both kindergarten and first grade are much less likely to be reading at grade level by third grade, and students not reading at grade level by third grade are four times more likely to drop out of high school; by sixth grade chronic absenteeism becomes one of the leading indicators that a student will drop out, and by high school attendance is a better dropout indicator than test scores (Framework for Action, ERIC ED617466, 2017) · grouping statement: student-weighted rate per grade, summing chronically absent students and contributing students across all school rows for that grade in 2022-23 · source line: NYC Open Data (sgsi-66kk), 2022-23
- **Visualization**: grade-curve — a filled series across the ordered K-12 grade axis with percent labels at both peaks and the trough, explicit axis titles for grade and percent of students chronically absent. Native-ready: grade-curve=yes

#### Slide 06 - Four reasons, and only one of them is "won't"

- **Audience move**: Frames absence as families not caring → holds four named causes, three of which are conditions the district can act on directly.
- **Relationships**: Barriers, aversions, disengagement, and myths are four members of one cause set (membership); each pairs with the district lever that answers it (link); the popular explanation stands in contrast to all four.
- **Composition**: Four equal cause groups in one band with the illustration held in a framed panel to one side; deliberately lower density than the pages around it.
- **Title**: Students miss school for four kinds of reasons
- **Core message**: Researchers group the causes as barriers, aversions, disengagement, and myths — which is why the answer is a system of supports rather than a message about caring more.
- **Content**: Barriers — unmanaged chronic health conditions such as asthma, diabetes, obesity and related illness, seizure disorders, mental health and anxiety, and vision problems; transport and household instability · Aversions — bullying, undiagnosed disabilities, overly punitive discipline · Disengagement — negative school climate or the absence of a supportive relationship at school · Myths — fears such as lice, and the belief that an excused absence does not count · one health scale marker: the Environmental Protection Agency estimates 10.5 million school days are missed each year because of asthma · framing line: three of the four are conditions a district can change; none of the four is solved by a letter home · source line: Framework for Action, ERIC ED617466, 2017
- **Images**: morning_threshold.jpg in a framed panel beside the cause groups, captioned as an illustration rather than a photograph of any school.

### Part 2: The recommendation

#### Slide 07 - Our recommendation

- **Audience move**: Waiting to hear what is actually being proposed → holds one sentence: a three-tier attendance system, built in two school years, run by district staff.
- **Relationships**: The three tiers are nested levels of one system (parent), applied in order of escalating need (order); each tier's population is a subset of the one below it (membership).
- **Composition**: The recommendation sentence dominant at the top, the three tiers appearing as one nested figure beneath it, with the tier ramp introduced here and reused for the rest of the deck.
- **Title**: Build one attendance system with three tiers, and staff it inside the district
- **Core message**: Prevention for everyone, early intervention for the students slipping, and coordinated case support for the students in crisis — one system, three intensities, run by Riverbend staff with Lantern building it.
- **Content**: The recommendation in one sentence · Tier 1, universal prevention, all students — attendance expectations, positive messaging, climate, and daily data visible to every teacher · Tier 2, early intervention, students approaching 10% — early-warning triggers, family contact, and a named adult · Tier 3, intensive support, students already chronically absent — case review, home visit, and community partner referral · why tiers: the framework's most effective approach is a comprehensive multi-tiered system of supports combining prevention with early and consistent interventions (ERIC ED617466, 2017) · what Lantern does versus what Riverbend does, stated as one line each · illustrative marker on the staffing figures
- **Motion suggestion**: The recommendation sentence is present first; the three tiers arrive from Tier 1 outward to Tier 3 so the nesting is read as escalation, and the figure holds its position for the next page.
- **Data class: scenario** — the staffing split between Lantern and Riverbend is illustrative.

#### Slide 08 - What each tier actually does, and where it stops

- **Audience move**: Accepts the tier idea in principle → can name the trigger, the owner, and the escalation boundary for each tier, including the point at which the district does not escalate.
- **Relationships**: Each tier pairs a trigger, an action, and an owner (link); the three tiers stay ordered by intensity (order); the legal boundary sits outside all three as an explicit limit (contrast).
- **Composition**: The same tier figure as the previous page, now expanded so each tier carries its trigger, action, and owner; the escalation boundary stated as a separate marked block.
- **Title**: Every tier has a trigger, an owner, and a stopping point
- **Core message**: The system escalates on data, not on frustration — and support is the response at every tier, with legal action treated as a very last resort.
- **Content**: Tier 1 — trigger: enrolled; action: attendance expectations set at enrollment, positive attendance messaging, daily attendance visible to every teacher; owner: site principal · Tier 2 — trigger: a student passes the early-warning threshold or misses a set number of days in a rolling window; action: same-week family contact, a named adult, a barrier check covering health, transport, and housing; owner: site attendance lead · Tier 3 — trigger: already chronically absent or referred by the team; action: case review, home visit, community partner referral, attendance plan with the family; owner: district attendance coordinator · the boundary: legal action is used only as a very last resort (ERIC ED617466, 2017) · comparative note: England's statutory guidance takes the same shape — schools and local authorities support first, and legal interventions apply only where attendance support does not work, is not engaged with, or is not appropriate (Department for Education, Working together to improve school attendance, first published 6 May 2022) · illustrative marker on the thresholds and role titles
- **Motion suggestion**: The tier figure carries over unchanged from the previous page and only the trigger, action, and owner lines resolve inside it; the boundary block arrives last.
- **Data class: scenario** — the thresholds, role titles, and response times are illustrative.

#### Slide 09 - Year one: build the system that notices

- **Audience move**: Wonders whether year one is discovery theatre → sees four dated phases, each ending in something the district can inspect.
- **Relationships**: Four phases run in sequence (order); each phase depends on the previous one's output (link); every phase has a district-side dependency (parent).
- **Composition**: One horizontal timeline across the page with four phase nodes connected in sequence, deliverables under each node, and the district's own obligations on a second line beneath.
- **Title**: Year one ends with an early-warning system Riverbend runs itself
- **Core message**: Four phases in ten months: baseline, build, pilot at three sites, then district-wide Tier 1 and Tier 2 — each phase ending in a deliverable the board can see.
- **Content**: Phase 1, Sep-Nov 2026, Baseline and root cause — attendance data audit, barrier survey, site interviews; deliverable: a baseline report with a root-cause profile per site · Phase 2, Dec 2026-Feb 2027, Design and build — early-warning thresholds, the teacher-facing dashboard specification, the family contact playbook; deliverable: the attendance operating manual · Phase 3, Mar-May 2027, Pilot at three schools — Tier 1 and Tier 2 live, weekly case review; deliverable: pilot results against the baseline · Phase 4, Jun-Aug 2027, District-wide rollout preparation — training all site attendance leads; deliverable: trained leads at every site · district dependencies named under each phase: data access, released staff time, a single executive sponsor · illustrative marker on every date and deliverable
- **Motion suggestion**: The timeline spine is present first, then the four phase nodes arrive in order along it; the spine keeps its position for the next page.
- **Data class: scenario** — every date, phase, and deliverable is illustrative.

#### Slide 10 - Year two, and the point where you can stop

- **Audience move**: Fears an open-ended engagement → sees the same timeline extended once, with a decision gate the board itself owns and a defined exit.
- **Relationships**: Year two continues the same ordered sequence (order); the gate sits between the two years and governs whether year two proceeds (parent); the hand-off endpoint contrasts with an open-ended retainer.
