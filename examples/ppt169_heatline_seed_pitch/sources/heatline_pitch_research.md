# Heat Pump Installer Capacity — Research Supplement

## Research Brief

- **Baseline supplied by the requester**: none. The request is topic-only: a seed-round investor pitch for "Heatline", a fictional company selling capacity-planning and job-scheduling software to residential heat-pump installers. All company, product, team, customer, traction, revenue, pipeline and financial figures are invented by the content owner and are labelled illustrative; none of them appear in this supplement.
- **Declared gaps** (external, verifiable claims the pitch cannot invent):
  1. G1 — Why now: the direction and size of the heat-pump market shift, 2022–2035.
  2. G2 — Policy: the demand-side subsidy and regulatory picture in the EU, UK and US, including what has just changed.
  3. G3 — The problem: evidence that installer capacity, not consumer demand, constrains deployment.
  4. G4 — Market sizing inputs: installation volumes, workforce size, and published software price points usable in a bottom-up derivation.
  5. G5 — Competition: what categories of tooling installers use today, described from public price and capability evidence rather than vendor claims.
- **External-source boundary**: external enrichment allowed; there is no supplied material to preserve or conflict with.
- **Output language**: English.
- **Audience / intent**: seed-stage investors; the deck must argue a market shift and a capacity bottleneck from checkable public evidence, and keep every company-specific number visibly illustrative.

## G1 — Why now: the market shift

The heat-pump market did not grow smoothly into 2025; it fell hard and then turned. Global sales declined 1% in 2024 (F001) after Europe, the third-largest market, dropped 21% — the largest decline ever recorded (F002), with Germany down almost 50% and France down 25% (F003). The United States moved the other way in the same year, up 15%, with heat pumps outselling natural-gas furnaces by 30% (F004).

2025 is the inflection the pitch is built on. Global sales fell about 2% (F005), but European annual sales rose 11% — the first year of growth since 2022 (F006) — driven by Germany, where first-half sales rose 55% (F007). It was also the first year heat pumps outsold gas boilers in Europe (F008).

The headroom is not in dispute: heat pumps still meet less than 15% of building heating demand worldwide (F009). Europe accounts for more than 20% of global sales by capacity (F010) and its installed stock is projected to nearly double by 2035 on current policies (F011). In the United States more than 20% of households already use a heat pump as their primary heating system (F012). China leads manufacturing with about 35% of global capacity and held roughly one-third of heat-pump patents in 2022 (F036), and Germany alone has around 900 MW of district-heating heat-pump capacity under construction or in planning (F035). The substitution is already visible on the other side of the meter: gas demand in buildings fell 12% between 2019 and 2024 (F019).

**Material conflict flagged**: the 2024/2025 direction reverses depending on region and year. A pitch that quotes only "the market is growing" or only "the market fell 21%" misrepresents the evidence. Both years belong on the why-now page.

## G2 — Policy: the demand side is legislated, and it just changed

The EU has removed the alternative rather than only subsidising the replacement. Under the recast EPBD, Article 17(15) requires Member States to discontinue any financial incentive for installing new stand-alone fossil-fuel boilers from 1 January 2025 at the latest (F013), and national building-renovation plans must set out a complete phase-out of fossil-fuel boilers by 2040 (F014).

The UK pays a direct grant: £7,500 towards an air-source or ground-source heat pump and £5,000 towards a biomass boiler (F015), with a further £1,500 available until March 2027 for off-gas-grid properties heated by oil or LPG (F016). The scheme has moved real volume: to 31 July 2026 it had received 151,136 voucher applications, issued 101,029 vouchers, redeemed 93,364, and paid £661,303,000 in grants (F017). The UK passed 250,000 certified heat-pump installations in February 2026, with 2025 the strongest year on record at 34% above 2024 and the Boiler Upgrade Scheme funding 43% of that year's installations (F034).

The United States moved the opposite way. The section 25C energy-efficient home-improvement credit is not allowed for any property placed in service after 31 December 2025 (F018).

**Reading for the pitch**: policy support is now geographically split. Europe's demand floor is legislated through 2040; the US retail incentive is gone. A software company selling to installers should locate itself where the installed base is being forced to grow.

## G3 — The problem: capacity, not demand

The bottleneck is people, and the gap is quantified by the European Commission itself: an estimated 750,000 more installers are needed, and at least 50% of existing installers will have to be reskilled to work with heat pumps (F020). Against that, EU installations ran at 2.8 million units in 2022, 2.7 million in 2023, 2.11 million in 2024 and 2.34 million in 2025 on preliminary data from 13 Member States (F021).

The trade body's figures point the same way. EHPA has stated Europe needs a minimum of 500,000 skilled full-time-equivalent employees by 2030 (F022) against roughly 117,000 then employed across the European heat-pump industry, about 37% of them in manufacturing rather than installation (F023) — and that reaching the target means installing 7 million heat pumps a year by 2030, up from about 2 million in 2021 (F024). A shortage of skilled installers, planners and technicians remains one of the main bottlenecks to scaling deployment (F032).

Globally, nearly 800,000 people work principally on heat pumps (F025), a workforce projected to grow 30% by 2035 on current policies, 60% in the stated-policies scenario and up to 140% in the net-zero scenario (F026).

The UK numbers are the sharpest illustration of the ratio: roughly 3,000 heat-pump engineers (F027) against at least 27,000 qualified engineers needed by 2028 and 62,000 by 2035 (F028), requiring 5,000–7,000 installers trained per year from 2025 to 2035 (F029).

Most importantly for a scheduling product, installers say so themselves. In a survey of 345 UK installers, 30% named finding suitable additional staff as the biggest barrier to doing more installations, rising to 41% among firms with six or more staff (F030); 81% of those larger firms said they were likely or very likely to employ new staff within twelve months (F031).

**Material conflict flagged**: in the same survey, lack of customer demand was cited more often overall (41%) than staffing (30%). The honest reading is that staffing is the binding constraint for firms that already have work — the segment a capacity-planning tool is sold to — not for the whole trade. The deck must not present F030 as "the top barrier for all installers".

## G4 — Market-sizing inputs

A bottom-up derivation can be built from published numbers only: EU annual installation volume (F021), the workforce required and its current size (F020, F022, F023, F025), the UK installer-count trajectory (F027, F028), and a per-seat software price anchor from a vendor that publishes list prices. Jobber's published plans are Core at $49/month for one user, Connect at $139, Grow at $199 for five users and Plus at $499 for five users, with additional users at $29/month (F033).

Note for the content owner: F033 is a price *anchor*, not a market size. Any "software spend per installer per year" figure derived from it is a construction of the presenter and must carry the illustrative marker.

## G5 — Competition

The public evidence supports describing categories rather than ranking named vendors. One category publishes transparent per-seat list pricing aimed at small trade businesses (F033). The generic field-service category exists and is adjacent, but public list pricing for the enterprise-grade tools is not uniformly published, so the deck should not assert a specific competitor's price or margin. The honest competitive claim available from public evidence is about *fit*: general field-service tooling is priced and designed per seat for dispatching jobs, while the constraint the evidence describes is a workforce that must be grown and reskilled (F020, F026, F029) — a capacity-planning problem over months, not a dispatching problem over days.
