# 01_executive_synthesis

The governing question is whether this firm should move to a four-day week on the 100-80-100 basis, and the answer this memo defends is a bounded pilot in two practice groups rather than a firm-wide commitment. Three things make that answer credible. The wellbeing effect is large and it survives control groups: burnout fell from 2.8 to 2.34 and stress from 3.07 to 2.74 across the UK cohort, and a controlled study of nearly 2,900 workers in 141 organisations across six countries found the same gains absent in its controls. The business case is a different matter: revenue rose 1.4 percent and resignations fell from 2.0 to 0.8 per hundred employees, but the pilot's own report states those trends cannot be called statistically significant. And professional services is where the model has been observed to fail. The gap to admit, if asked, is scale: 37 percent of the UK cohort had ten staff or fewer, and only a tenth were above a hundred people.

# 02_situation_complication

This page settles what is actually on the table. The 100-80-100 principle was created in 2018 by Andrew Barnes and Charlotte Lockhart at Perpetual Guardian, and 4 Day Week Global defines it as a 32-hour week rather than compressed hours, so a compressed model is a different intervention. Against the national norm the cut is smaller than the headline suggests: average actual weekly hours for UK full-time workers were 36.7 in April to June 2026, which makes 32 hours roughly a 13 percent reduction rather than 20. The complication is that no measured pilot delivered its nominal week. The UK cohort went from 38 hours to 34, Portugal from over 41 to 36.5, and South Cambridgeshire from 37 to 32 — the only one that reached the target. Seventy-one percent of UK employees reported fewer hours and fifteen percent reported working more. So the real question is how many hours this firm can recover through redesign, and what removing the rest costs in client service.

# 03_driver_decomposition

Four conditions decide whether 100-80-100 holds here, and the page separates the three we own from the one we do not. Recoverable hours came, in every pilot, from shorter and fewer meetings, reformed email etiquette, protected focus periods and automated reporting; the German participants named meeting culture and reduced distraction as their main levers. Redesign effort is the strongest predictor in the whole evidence base: only 8 percent of Portuguese companies that made two or more organisational changes reverted to five days, against 38 percent of those that made none or one. Retention economics look attractive but must be handled carefully — resignations fell from 2.0 to 0.8 per hundred in the UK pilot, and the most-cited UK replacement cost of thirty thousand six hundred and fourteen pounds is a 2014 figure that is not inflation-adjusted, so any saving derived from it is arithmetic, not a finding. Client tolerance is the branch we cannot prepare our way out of.

# 04_evidence_diagnosis

This is the page to hold on to when someone quotes a headline. What the evidence establishes is worker wellbeing: the six-country controlled study found gains in burnout, job satisfaction and mental and physical health that its control organisations did not show, and the German pilot, instrumented with within-organisation controls, smartwatches and cortisol samples, found the same falls in stress and burnout. What it does not establish is the business case. The UK 2022 report states in its own words that its resignation, new-hire and absence trends cannot be called statistically significant, given small samples and labour-market contingencies, and Germany found sick days, turnover and profit changed only slightly and not significantly. That is why the two grey bars on the chart carry that label at the same size as the number. The methodological pattern behind all of it — self-selection, self-reported financials, absent or small control arms, shrinking follow-up samples — is documented in the primary reports themselves, not asserted by critics.

# 05_cohort_persistence

Persistence is the most-quoted evidence for the four-day week, and it holds up better than the business metrics, provided each rate is read with its base. Fifty-six of the sixty-one UK companies, or 92 percent, were still running the policy at the end of the trial, and at twelve months at least fifty-four, or 89 percent, still were. In Germany, 73 percent were still on reduced hours after the pilot, while a fifth returned to a five-day forty-hour week and two large organisations dropped out early citing economic reasons. The 100 percent figure from the 2024 to 2025 UK pilot rests on seventeen self-selected companies, which is a weaker signal than 89 percent on sixty-one, and this memo deliberately does not lead with it. Two qualifications matter for us. Nearly half the permanent cases never changed contracts, so the entitlement can be withdrawn — and universal or staggered day-off models converted to permanence far more often than flexible ones.

# 06_study_comparability

Put on one basis, most of the precedents the committee will hear quoted are about a different intervention or a different sector. Iceland moved public-sector workers from forty hours to thirty-five or thirty-six, and Anthony Veal records that in sixty-one of sixty-six workplaces the reduction was only one to three hours, with no monitored control. Belgium's Labour Deal is a right to compress thirty-eight hours into four days at unchanged total hours and pay, which is not 100-80-100 at all. Portugal has the strongest design, including a control group of interested non-adopters, but its own authors call it a proof of concept and say its participants are not representative. Germany has the strongest method but many participants simply cut hours across five days. South Cambridgeshire is a council delivering statutory services. That leaves the UK cohort and its twelve-month follow-up as the only evidence about firms like ours — and the two conflicts at the foot of the page are shown, not reconciled.

# 07_recommendation

The recommendation is deliberately narrow: two whole practice groups, about sixty people, on full pay with a universal day off and a target thirty-two-hour week, after ten weeks of redesign, running for six months with one interim gate and no change to contracts. Each design choice traces back to the evidence. Redesign comes first because reversion tracks redesign effort and two to three months of preparation is the consistent figure across the UK, Portuguese and six-country studies. A universal day off is chosen because universal and staggered models converted to permanence in 95 percent of cases against 60 percent for flexible ones. Metrics are agreed before the start because half the Portuguese companies could not define productivity metrics at all. And the pilot is stated in writing as non-contractual, because nearly half the permanent cases elsewhere never changed contracts. Three things are explicitly not recommended: a firm-wide commitment, a compressed week, and an hours cut spread across five days.

# 08_roadmap

The roadmap exists to make the decision reversible, and to fix the points of reversal before anyone becomes attached to the policy. Phase zero runs ten weeks under the chief operating officer and does the unglamorous work: cutting meeting length and frequency, reforming email etiquette, agreeing the metric set, and agreeing what we will tell clients — Portugal used eight preparation sessions over three months on exactly those questions. Phase one operates for six months under the practice heads. Gate A at month three asks whether the kill criteria are holding, and it should be read as the high-water mark rather than the trend, because most Portuguese indicators slipped between three and six months for reasons the authors could not fully explain. Phase two evaluates in month seven against the non-participating practice groups as a control — the design Portugal had and the UK 2022 pilot lacked. Gate B decides. Owners, dates and phase lengths on this page are illustrative.

# 09_risk_mitigation

Six risks, and the matrix is arranged so the committee can see that only one of them sits in the top-right cell. Client and stakeholder expectations is that risk, and it is the one preparation cannot remove, because it depends on how clients react rather than on how well we plan; it is also precisely what stopped the only comparable firm in the UK cohort, and what deterred participation in Belgium's reduced-hours pilot. The rest are preparation problems with owners against them: hours creep, which we control by measuring actual hours rather than the policy; the absence of an agreed productivity definition, fixed in phase zero; benefit fade after month three; a dispute when the policy is withdrawn, answered by stating its non-contractual status in writing; and inequitable application, answered by taking whole practice groups rather than individual opt-in. One honest caveat: the risks are sourced, but the likelihood and impact ratings are our judgement, not findings.

# 10_appendix_evidence

This page exists so that nothing in the memo has to be taken on trust. Every figure used above traces to one of twenty-seven sources, each linked here with its publisher, its period and the pages that rely on it. Four limitations apply to all of them and are worth repeating in the room: management self-selects participation, company financial data is self-reported, control arms are absent, small or non-random, and follow-up samples shrink. The definitions matter too — continuation means the policy is still operating, which is not the same as a contractual entitlement, and not statistically significant is the reporting authors' own wording rather than a critic's. Three things are deliberately not claimed anywhere: any figure for this firm, any productivity forecast, and any saving from the 2014 replacement-cost figure as more than illustrative arithmetic. Where two credible sources disagree, on Iceland and on South Cambridgeshire, both readings are shown.
