## Research Brief

**Requested outcome.** An English-language A4 print decision memo answering a single question for an executive committee: should a mid-size professional-services firm — 200 people, UK, illustrative and unnamed — move to a four-day week on the 100-80-100 basis (100% pay, 80% time, 100% output)? The memo must argue honestly and be checkable, presenting supporting evidence and critical, null and negative evidence side by side, so the committee can reach a go/no-go decision rather than be sold one.

**Supplied-material baseline.** None. No client material, internal data, prior analysis or house position was supplied. Every externally verifiable claim below was researched from scratch, and every one carries a source in the companion facts file.

**Audience and communication intent.** An executive committee taking a reversible but expensive operating decision. It needs magnitudes, periods and provenance, not enthusiasm; it needs to know where the evidence is strong, where it is self-reported, and where it does not exist at all. Nothing here has been researched about the firm itself, and nothing here should be presented as evidence about the firm.

**Declared gaps researched.** (1) The UK 2022 pilot and its primary report; (2) the 2024 twelve-month follow-up; (3) Iceland 2015-2019; (4) Belgium's 2022 law; (5) Portugal 2023-24; (6) critical, null and negative evidence; (7) professional-services-specific evidence and UK context numbers; (8) implementation-design evidence. All eight are covered. 72 externally sourced facts (F001-F072) across 27 distinct sources.

**Scope boundary.** No financial model, no firm-specific projection and no recommendation is contained here — this is the evidence base for the memo, not the memo.

## Gap 1 — The UK 2022 pilot (the anchor study)

The pilot ran June to December 2022 with 61 companies and around 2,900 workers, and its authors describe it as the largest four-day week trial to that date [F001]. Companies got two months of structured preparation before day one — workshops, coaching, mentoring and peer support [F002]. The only conditions were that pay stayed at 100% and that employees received a "meaningful" reduction in work time; no single model was imposed [F003], so companies used classic Friday-off, staggered, decentralised, annualised and conditional structures [F004]. Research was led by Autonomy with quantitative analysis by Boston College and University College Dublin and qualitative work by Cambridge and Salford [F020].

**Continuation.** At the end of the trial 56 of 61 companies (92%) were continuing and 18 had made it permanent [F005].

**Business metrics.** Revenue rose 1.4% on a size-weighted average from trial start to end across the 23 companies with usable data, and 34.5% against a comparable six-month period a year earlier across a different set of 24 companies [F006]. Headcount was effectively flat, down 1.3% across 34 organisations [F007]. Resignations fell from 2.0 to 0.8 per 100 employees (57%) and new hires from 3.4 to 2.4 (37%) [F008]. Absence — sick and personal days per employee per month — fell from 2.0 to 0.7, a 65% reduction [F009].

**The caveat the headlines drop.** The report itself states that, because of small sample numbers and labour-market contingencies, the resignation, new-hire and absence trends cannot be called statistically significant [F010]. This is the single most important qualification in the entire evidence base and belongs in the memo next to the 57% figure, not in a footnote.

**Hours actually worked.** Average work time fell from 38 hours to 34, not to 32, because some firms cut less than eight hours and some staff still worked on the fifth day; 71% of employees reported fewer hours but 15% reported working more [F011].

**Employee outcomes.** Burnout fell from 2.8 to 2.34 on a 1-5 composite, with 71% improving and 22% worsening [F012]. Stress fell from 3.07 to 2.74 (39% better, 13% worse) and job satisfaction rose from 7.12 to 7.69 [F013]. Mental health rose 2.95 to 3.32 and anxiety fell 2.26 to 1.96; 37% reported better physical health against 18% worse, and fatigue fell 2.56 to 2.12 [F014]. Sleep difficulties fell for 40%, were unchanged for 45% and rose for 15% [F015]. Work-life measures improved: 60% found care responsibilities easier, 62% social life, 54% household jobs [F016]. Asked what pay rise would buy them back to five days, 46% said 0-25%, 29% said 26-50%, 8% said over 50% and 15% said no amount of money would [F017].

**Cohort composition — read before generalising.** The largest sector was marketing/advertising (eight firms) and the second was professional services (seven), with charities third [F018]; but the cohort was overwhelmingly tiny employers, 37% with 1-10 staff and 29% with 11-25, only 10% above 100 people [F018]. A 200-person firm is at the extreme upper end of this evidence, not in its middle. Only 44-51 of the 61 organisations supplied survey data for the performance analysis, all company metrics were self-reported, and employee survey response fell from 77% at baseline to 58% at endpoint [F019].

## Gap 2 — The twelve-month follow-up (2024)

One year on, at least 54 of the 61 original organisations (89%) were still running a four-day week; five (8%) had discontinued and two did not respond [F022]. At least 31 (51%) had made it permanent, up from 18 [F023]. The follow-up research itself rests on 28 organisations — under half the cohort — that agreed to participate [F024]. Of those 28: 39% permanent with updated terms and conditions, 39% permanent without changing contracts (an opt-in or "gift" policy), 18% still on an extended trial, 4% discontinued [F025]. Every consulted manager and CEO called the impact positive, 55% "very positive"; 82% reported wellbeing gains, 50% turnover gains and 32% recruitment gains [F026]. A separate staff survey across 47 of the original organisations found the health, work-life-balance, life-satisfaction and burnout improvements had held, with work intensity lower and job satisfaction higher than before the 2022 pilot began [F027].

Pay and hours held in the sense that continuation was defined as still operating the policy; note that in nearly half the permanent cases contracts were never changed [F025], which means the entitlement can be withdrawn.

**The most relevant single data point in the whole file.** The one organisation among the 28 that discontinued was a small consultancy. It reported morale, wellbeing and efficiency benefits, yet stopped — because it could not manage client and stakeholder expectations in an industry that had not adopted working-time reduction, and could not apply the policy equitably across a small team [F028]. That is a professional-services firm failing on exactly the axis a professional-services firm would fail on.

Implementation model mattered: universal or staggered day-off models converted to permanent in 95% of cases against 60% for more flexible models, though the authors note the cohort is too small for statistical significance [F029].

## Gap 3 — Iceland 2015-2019, and why it is weaker than it is quoted as being

Two trials, initiated by Reykjavik City Council and the national government, ran 2015-2019; Reykjavik grew to over 2,500 staff and the government trial covered around 440, together more than 1% of the working population, with many workers moving from 40 hours to 35 or 36 without pay reduction [F030]. Productivity and service provision stayed the same or improved across the majority of trial workplaces, and the trials were revenue neutral for both the council and the government [F031]. By June 2021 roughly 86% of Iceland's working population had either moved to shorter hours or won the right to, through contracts negotiated 2019-2021 [F032].

**The counter-case, and it is material.** Anthony Veal (University of Technology Sydney) argues these were not four-day week experiments at all: across 66 workplaces and about 2,500 workers the shift was 40 hours to 35 or 36, and in 61 of the 66 workplaces the reduction was only one to three hours; the national agreements that followed formalised 35 minutes a week in the private sector and 65 minutes in the public sector [F033]. He also flags the Hawthorne effect, volunteer participants motivated to make it work, and the absence of a monitored control sample [F034].

**Material conflict.** Iceland is routinely cited in four-day week advocacy as proof at national scale. Two credible readings of the same trials disagree not about the numbers but about what they were: a transformative shorter-week success [F030, F031, F032] versus a modest 1-4 hour reduction whose national legacy is under an hour a week [F033]. The memo should carry both and let the committee discount accordingly; using Iceland as headline support without F033 would be the most likely way this memo loses credibility in the room.

## Gap 4 — Belgium 2022: compression, not reduction

Belgium's Labour Deal permits a full-time week of in principle 38 hours to be worked in four days instead of five, with daily hours rising to 9.5 (10 for a 40-hour week, requiring a collective agreement). Total weekly hours and pay are unchanged. The employee must request it in writing; the request lasts six months and is renewable; an employer refusing must give written justification within one month [F035]. The provisions took effect on 20 November 2022, and employees may request them even where no work rules or collective agreement implement them [F036].

**This must be stated explicitly in the memo.** Belgium is a right to compress, not a reduction in hours, and is not evidence for a 100-80-100 move. On take-up, a Belgian Federal Planning Bureau and Ghent University study of reduced working time, funded by the Belgian Science Policy Office, found only one company fully participated in its pilot project, citing lack of awareness, legal uncertainty around wage legislation, and concerns about reputation, teamwork and sectoral expectations [F037]. Sectoral expectation is the same barrier that stopped the UK consultancy in F028.

## Gap 5 — Portugal 2023-24: the best-designed pilot, and the most candid about itself

Origin was a political initiative of the Ministry of Labour, Solidarity and Social Security of the XXIII Portuguese Government, coordinated by Pedro Gomes (Birkbeck) and Rita Fontinha (Henley Business School) with technical support from 4 Day Week Global; the final report is dated July 2024 [F038]. It covered 41 companies and more than 1,000 workers — 21 starting a coordinated six-month test in June 2023 alongside 20 earlier adopters — on three principles: no salary cuts, a genuine hours reduction, voluntary participation. Most cut a 40-hour week to 36, 34 or 32 hours, and firms offering only a free Friday afternoon were excluded [F039].

**Why it is the strongest design.** It included a control group of workers from companies that were interested but chose not to reduce hours. Actual weekly hours in the pilot group fell 12%, from over 41 to 36.5, and the share working 40+ hours fell from 79% to 20%, with no reduction in the control group [F040].

**Outcomes.** The share rating their mental health "very good" or "excellent" doubled from 15% to 30%, physical health rose from 20% to 27%, sleep rose 11 minutes a night, and 93% of workers wanted the trial to continue [F041]. At three months anxiety was down 21%, fatigue 23% and sleep problems 19%; the share struggling to balance work and family fell from 46% to 8% and 65% spent more time with family [F042]. Workers valued the four-day week at about 28% of salary [F049].

**Null and negative findings from the same report.** Most indicators slipped slightly between three and six months, which the authors tentatively attribute to seasonality but cannot fully explain [F043]. After six months, four participants returned to five days and five scaled the policy down [F044]. Half the companies found it difficult to define productivity metrics at all and over a third struggled to change the internal culture around time management; only one firm (a nursery) needed more staff, a 4.5% increase, about 40% of managers identified savings and almost 80% rated the project financially neutral [F047]. And the report states plainly that because no financial incentives were offered, participants are not representative of typical Portuguese companies and the study serves only as a "Proof of Concept" [F046].

**The most actionable finding anywhere in this file.** Only 8% of companies that made two or more organisational changes reverted to five days, against 38% of those that made none or one; 75% made at least one change, most commonly shorter meetings, and 87% reported active worker participation in redesigning processes [F045]. Reversion tracks redesign effort, not sector or size.

## Gap 6 — Critical, null and negative evidence

**Germany 2024 — the pilot with instruments.** 45 organisations across consulting, services, manufacturing, care, IT and media, six months from 1 February 2024, scientifically directed by Prof. Julia Backmann and Dr Felix Hoch at the University of Munster with the consultancy Intraprenor, using within-organisation control groups, smartwatches and cortisol hair samples. Participants slept 38 minutes longer per week than controls, were more active, and showed significant falls in stress and burnout and substantially improved life satisfaction [F050]. But monthly sick days fell only slightly and not significantly; turnover and profit rose slightly but not significantly against the previous year; there was no measurable environmental benefit and domestic travel increased [F051]. There was no strong evidence of any positive change in overall job satisfaction, and many organisations reduced hours across five days rather than moving to a genuine four-day schedule [F052]. Afterwards 34% extended the pilot, 39% ended the trial but kept reduced hours, and 20% went back to a five-day 40-hour week; two large organisations dropped out early citing economic reasons [F053].

**South Cambridgeshire District Council — the only UK case with independent statistical evaluation and hostile scrutiny.** The trial began 1 January 2023 for desk-based services and 19 September 2023 for the Shared Waste Service; from 1 April 2024 hours were harmonised at 32 a week, 86.5% of contracted hours, with pay maintained [F054]. Evaluation was by Dr Joshua Pink and Prof. Daiga Kamerade (Salford) and Prof. Brendan Burchell (Cambridge) [F055]. Adjusting for COVID, 11 outcomes were significantly better — calls handled, complaints in time, invoices paid in 30 days, benefit change processing, staff turnover, emergency repairs in 24 hours and five planning measures — and two were significantly worse: the percentage of housing rent collected and the average days to re-let housing stock [F056]. For everything else no statistically significant difference could be found, which the authors stress does not mean nothing changed; and they state the analysis alone cannot prove the trial caused any of the changes [F057].

**Material conflict, and the memo should show it.** The council's own July 2025 communications report 21 of 24 services improved or stayed the same, leavers down over 40%, applications up over 120% and a yearly saving of nearly GBP 400,000 [F058]. The independent statistical evaluation reports 11 better, 2 worse and the rest indistinguishable [F056]. These are not the same claim: "improved or stayed the same" counts non-significant results as wins, while the statistical evaluation refuses to. Both are honest; only one is evidence of causation.

**Government position.** MHCLG issued a Best Value Notice in November 2023, reissued it on 8 May 2024, let it expire on 8 November 2024 and did not reissue it — while stating it is not government policy to support a general move to a four-day week for five days' pay, and recognising local authorities as independent employers [F059]. Relevant to a private firm mainly as a signal about external legitimacy, not as a constraint.

**The economic objection, properly stated.** David Eiser and Mark Mitchell of the Fraser of Allander Institute argue that cutting hours without commensurate productivity gains, and without cutting pay, means lower profits or higher public funding, and that in health, social care, education, personal services and the arts the scope for productivity improvement is limited; they note the cross-country association between shorter hours and higher productivity is correlational, not causal [F060]. Their concrete counter-example is the EU Working Time Directive, where reduced hours could not be absorbed by productivity and the UK had to recruit more doctors through training and overseas hiring at non-negligible fiscal cost [F061]. This is the honest form of the output-per-hour versus total-output objection: a firm can raise output per hour by 25% and still lose total output if the ceiling on efficiency gain in its work is lower than that.

**The peer-reviewed evidence, and its own limits.** Fan, Schor, Kelly and Gu, "Work time reduction via a 4-day workweek finds improvements in workers' well-being", Nature Human Behaviour vol. 9(10), 2153-2168 (2025), analysed nearly 2,900 workers across 141 organisations in six countries and found improvements in burnout, job satisfaction, mental and physical health that were absent in control organisations [F062]. Two qualifications: the control arm was only 12 companies, and the actual reduction averaged about five hours a week rather than eight, after roughly eight weeks of pre-trial reorganisation [F063]. Note also what this paper is and is not: it is strong evidence on worker wellbeing, and it is not evidence on firm output or revenue.

**Standing methodological pattern across all of the above.** Participation is self-selected by management in every pilot; company financial data is self-reported by the participating firms; control arms are absent (UK 2022), small (12 companies, Nature Human Behaviour) or non-random (Portugal, Germany); and follow-up samples shrink (28 of 61 at twelve months). Each of these is documented in the primary reports themselves [F010, F019, F024, F040, F046, F050, F063] rather than asserted by critics — which is the strongest possible way to put the caveat to an executive committee.

## Gap 7 — Professional-services-specific evidence and UK context

**Direct sector evidence is thin and mixed.** Professional services was the second-largest sector in the UK 2022 pilot with seven firms, behind marketing/advertising with eight [F018] — but the pilot's only documented twelve-month failure was a small consultancy that stopped over client and stakeholder expectations and intra-team equity, despite reporting benefits [F028]. Portugal's participants included consulting firms, and consulting and IT were named as sectors where contracted hours understated actual hours worked [F040].

**Named firms with published outcomes.** Awin, an affiliate marketing network of 1,300+ people across 17 offices, ran an 18-month Flexi-Week trial from January 2021 and made it permanent on 23 February 2023, with staff choosing one full day off or two half-days at full salary; it reports regrettable turnover down 33%, sick leave down 21%, 85% of staff saying it influences their decision to stay, group gross profit growth averaging 13% a year 2019-2022 and NPS up 20.7 points [F065]. Atom Bank moved to a 34-hour four-day week with no pay loss in November 2021 and ten months in reported applications up 49% year on year, 91% of staff completing everything needed within four days, 92% encouraged to find efficiencies, and Trustpilot rising from 4.54 to 4.82 [F066]. Both are self-published company accounts, not independent evaluations, and should be labelled as such in the memo.

**Billable-hour firms specifically.** Two North American law firms run 80% of the work for 100% of the pay: YLaw in Vancouver (Leena Yousefi, 14 lawyers) and The Ross Firm (Quinn Ross, 18 lawyers), the latter reporting productivity up over 10%. The reported frictions are instructive and small-scale: email volume on return from days off, heavier HR administration, and job applicants misreading the model as working less [F067]. Both firms are an order of magnitude smaller than a 200-person firm; no large UK professional-services firm with published four-day week results was found.

**UK context numbers.** ONS Labour Force Survey data put average actual weekly hours for UK full-time workers at 36.7 in April to June 2026 [F068] — so a 32-hour week is roughly a 13% cut against the national full-time norm, not 20%, which matters for how the change is framed to clients and recruits. ONS international comparisons put UK output per hour at GBP 46.92 in 2021, 10% below the average of the other G7 nations excluding Japan, with output per worker 16% below [F069]. On the retention side, Oxford Economics research for Unum reported in February 2014 put the average cost of replacing a UK employee at GBP 30,614 — GBP 25,181 of lost output over an average 28 weeks to reach optimum productivity, plus GBP 5,433 of logistical cost [F070].

**Conflict to flag on the retention figure.** F070 is a 2014 figure and is the most-cited UK number of its kind; it is not inflation-adjusted and predates every pilot in this file. If the memo uses it to size a retention benefit, it must be dated in the text, and the resulting estimate must be presented as illustrative arithmetic rather than as a finding.

## Gap 8 — Implementation design

**The principle.** 100:80:100 means 100% pay for 80% of the time while maintaining 100% output. It was created in 2018 by Andrew Barnes and Charlotte Lockhart during a trial at Perpetual Guardian in New Zealand and is trademarked by 4 Day Week Global, which defines a four-day week as a 32-hour week rather than compressed hours [F064]. Belgium [F035] is therefore outside the definition, and the German pilot's five-day hours reductions [F052] sit at its edge.

**Models in use.** Universal or staggered day off, decentralised, annualised, conditional [F004]; 9-day fortnights appear in the 2024-25 UK pilot, where 12 of 17 companies took a full four-day week and five a nine-day fortnight [F071]. Universal and staggered models had the highest conversion to permanence at 95% against 60% for more flexible models [F029], though on a sample too small for significance.

**Preparation.** The UK 2022 pilot used two months of structured preparation [F002]; Portugal used eight sessions over three months, March to May 2023, covering format choice, communication with workers and with clients, evaluation metrics and process change [F048]; the Nature Human Behaviour cohort had roughly eight weeks of reorganisation [F063]. Two to three months is the consistent figure.

**What companies actually changed.** UK 2022 participants cut meeting length and frequency and imposed clearer agendas, reformed email etiquette, introduced protected focus periods, automated reporting and customer-service steps, consolidated project-management software, and in several cases asked staff to sign an "efficiency charter" committing to higher productivity as the condition of the shorter week [F021]. Germany's participants reported meeting-culture change and reduced distractions as the main efficiency levers [F050]. Portugal's most common single change was shorter meetings, and the reversion rate tracked the number of organisational changes made [F045].

**Latest UK data point.** The 4 Day Week Foundation's later pilot with Boston College covered 17 companies and nearly 1,000 workers over six months and ended with all 17 continuing a shorter week [F071]; 62% of workers recorded less frequent burnout, 41% better mental health and 45% higher life satisfaction [F072]. A 100% continuation rate on 17 self-selected companies is a weaker signal than 89% on 61 [F022], and the memo should say so rather than lead with it.

## Research notes on retrieval

**Fetching refusals and workarounds.** The peer-reviewed Nature Human Behaviour article and the Nature news article about it both returned an authentication redirect and could not be read directly; the bibliographic record and abstract were verified through the RePEc catalogue entry [F062], and the two figures available only from Nature's news coverage are marked as such [F063]. PubMed returned a cookie wall. The council committee document server refused with HTTP 403, so the South Cambridgeshire evidence was taken from the council's own published copy of the independent report [F055-F057] and from the committee paper on the city council's democracy portal [F054]. The American Bar Association, the World Economic Forum and the APA all refused or returned empty bodies; CNN returned HTTP 451 and ITV timed out, so the 2024-25 UK pilot figures come from the 4 Day Week Foundation's own project page [F071] and reputable reporting that cites it [F072]. Several council news slugs returned 404.

**One retrieval hazard worth recording.** The automated page-summarising step returned confidently worded but fabricated figures for one council PDF — a staff count, a KPI count and a savings figure that appear nowhere in the document. Every PDF-derived number in this file was therefore re-extracted from the downloaded document locally and read directly. Any number in the memo that cannot be traced to a fact ID here should be treated as unverified.

**Conflicts recorded above, in one place.** Iceland's significance (F030-F032 versus F033-F034); South Cambridgeshire's results as communicated versus as evaluated (F058 versus F056-F057); the UK 2022 revenue and retention gains versus the report's own statement that they are not statistically significant (F006, F008 versus F010); the pilot's sector percentages, which do not correspond to a 61-company base and appear computed on a smaller responding subset (F018); the gap between the nominal 32-hour week and the 34-36.5 hours actually worked across the UK, Portuguese and six-country studies (F011, F040, F063); and the 2014 vintage of the replacement-cost figure (F070).
