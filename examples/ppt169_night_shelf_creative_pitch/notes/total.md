# 01_cover

Thank you for making the time. Before anything else, one piece of housekeeping: Fog and Fern Books is a fictional shop, and every commercial figure in this deck — footfall, budget, staffing, print quantities — is illustrative. The research is not. Every number about reading and sleep is real and linked at the back. What you are about to see is a late autumn campaign called The Night Shelf, and the whole thing rests on a single idea: give the night back to reading.

---

# 02_audience_truth

Here is the truth the work is built on, and it is a truth about time, not about taste. The Bureau of Labor Statistics measures how Americans actually spend a day, and in the 2025 averages, reading for personal interest comes to about sixteen minutes. Television comes to two hours and thirty-seven. That is the same day. Look at how the gap changes with age. Among fifteen to twenty-four year olds it is ten minutes of reading against one hour forty-six of television. Among people sixty-five and over, reading more than triples, but television nearly doubles as well. Nobody in this room has to argue that people should love books more. They already do. What they do not have is room.

---

# 03_the_last_hour

So where is the room? There is exactly one place left, and something else is already sitting in it. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine surveyed two thousand US adults last June: half of them use a screen in bed every single day, another third most days of the week, and a quarter openly say they choose the phone over the sleep they know they need. And that hour has a measurable price. A Norwegian study of forty-five thousand students, published in Frontiers in Psychiatry in March, found that each additional hour of screen time after getting into bed raised the odds of insomnia symptoms by fifty-nine percent and cost twenty-four minutes of sleep. Now here is the part that turns a problem into a campaign. Reading in bed is the one behaviour that has been tested the other way. In a randomised trial of nine hundred and ninety-one people, forty-two percent of those who read a book in bed said their sleep improved, against twenty-eight percent who did not. The bookshop is not selling a nicer habit. It is selling a better one, and it can say so with a straight face.

---

# 04_the_task

Which gives the work a very specific job. Make Fog and Fern the shop a reader thinks of at nine in the evening. Not the shop they visit on a Saturday afternoon — the one that owns the last hour of their day. And it has to happen inside three constraints, all of which are illustrative for this example client but realistic for a shop your size. No paid media at all beyond the frontage and the channels you already own. One season, six weeks from the first paste-up to the last event. And five people, every one of whom is already working a full shift.

---

# 05_strategic_thought

The bridge is one sentence long, and it is the reason the constraints stop being a problem. Reading is not competing with the day. It is competing with the last hour. Stop arguing for reading in general and go and own bedtime. That single move makes a no-budget campaign winnable, because it tells you exactly where the work has to appear — the street at seven, the shelf at eight, the bedside at ten — and it tells you what it has to sound like.

---

# 06_the_idea

The Night Shelf. The last hour belongs to a book. That is the whole idea, and it is deliberately something a customer can repeat without being told twice. The shelf is real — a physical shelf in the shop, restocked every week with books chosen for the hour before sleep, staff-picked and short enough to finish. And the shop stays open into that hour, which is the part nobody else on the street is doing.

# 08_range_and_extension

It is a platform, not a poster, and here is the proof — four surfaces, four different moments of the same evening. The street poster catches the walk home, printed two colours on uncoated stock so it looks like something the shop made. The phone post arrives when the screen is already in your hand, which is the honest place to interrupt it. The window lightbox is the panel you already own, running the same picture at full width so someone crossing the road can read it. And the bookmark leaves with the book and shows up at the bedside, which is where the idea finally lands. All four are mockups of proposed executions, and none of them assumes a placement you do not already control.

---

# 09_identity_in_use

This is the system underneath, and it is small enough that you can keep running it yourself after we have gone. One type voice: a bookish serif for everything the campaign says, and a quiet sans for everything the shop adds later, so your own notices never look like they are shouting. Four colours, each doing exactly one job. Night ink is always the ground. Lamplight is the light in the picture and the mark — it is never used for type, which is the rule that keeps everything legible. Paper cream is anything a reader physically touches. Fern is only ever your own signature, so a customer learns to read it as the shop speaking rather than the campaign. And the crescent on the shelf rule works from a poster down to a bookmark without redrawing it. The one prohibition worth remembering: red only ever appears when we are talking about a screen.

---

# 10_tone_and_craft

A word about how the finished thing should feel, because that is easier to lose than the idea. Unhurried. Warm, printed, and made by the shop rather than bought by it. In practice that means three decisions. Uncoated cream stock, two inks, no laminate — it should feel like paper, not packaging. The photography happens at night in your shop with the lights you already have, not lit as a studio set. And every line gets written to be read aloud in one breath, because half of these will be spoken by a bookseller before anyone reads them.

---

# 11_environment

And this is the only test that actually matters for a shop with no media budget. At real size, from the pavement on the far side of the street, seen at ordinary standing height — does the poster still read? It does, because the composition is one shape and four words. This is a mockup, but it is at the window's real dimensions and at honest eye level. Nothing here assumes a site you do not already have.

---

# 12_production_reality

Now the unglamorous half, and I want to be straightforward about it. Six weeks: brief, artwork, print, paste-up, launch, then the event run. Seven deliverables across four channel classes, two print suppliers, and no paid media anywhere. Every quantity, lead time and owner on this page is illustrative for this fictional client and none of it is a quotation — but the shape is right, and the shape says this is buildable by five people who are already busy. The two things that genuinely constrain the timeline are the poster and the lightbox panel, both at three weeks, so artwork has to be signed off in week two or the whole run slides.

---

# 13_close

Finally, how you will know it worked, using only something you can already count. If the shop is busier between eight and ten in the evening in week six than it was in week one, the idea worked. No panel, no tracking study, no attribution model — just your own door in your own hour. So the ask is simple: approve The Night Shelf for late autumn. Everything on the research side is listed here and every link is live, and to say it once more plainly, Fog and Fern Books and every figure attached to it are fictional and exist only as an example.
