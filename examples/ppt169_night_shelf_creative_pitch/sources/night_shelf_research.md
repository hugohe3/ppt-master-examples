# The Night Shelf — Research Supplement

## Research Brief

- **Baseline supplied by the requester**: none. The request is topic-only: a creative campaign pitch for a fictional independent bookstore ("Fog & Fern Books", a mid-size English-speaking city) for a late-autumn campaign called "The Night Shelf", whose idea is to give the night back to reading. The client, the store, the campaign and every commercial figure attached to them are fictional.
- **Declared gaps** (all externally verifiable, none inventable):
  1. G1 — How much do adults actually read, and in what format?
  2. G2 — How does reading time compare with screen time in a real day?
  3. G3 — Is reading participation falling, and on what evidence?
  4. G4 — What happens at night specifically: how common is screen use in bed, and what does it cost sleep?
  5. G5 — Is there peer-reviewed evidence that reading in bed is a *better* night behaviour, not just a nicer one?
  6. G6 — What is the real state of the independent bookstore trade the fictional client sits in?
- **Research boundary**: external enrichment allowed and required; no supplied facts exist to preserve. Fictional client economics (footfall, budget, store count, sales) are invented for the pitch and must be marked illustrative on the page — they are never recorded here as facts.
- **Known audience / intent**: an agency-to-client creative pitch; the audience is the fictional store's owner and small team. Communication intent is persuasion built on a real audience truth.
- **Requested outcome**: a 12–14 page English pitch deck, ppt169, creative-pitch Style.
- **Output language**: English.

---

## G1 — How much do adults read, and in what format

Book reading in the United States is not a minority behaviour: three-quarters of adults touched at least one book in a year (F001). But the distribution is thin and print still leads by a wide margin (F002, F003). Shared reading — the book club — is rare (F004), which matters for a campaign that wants to make reading a public, in-store occasion rather than a private one.

- Reach is high, depth is shallow: F001, F003.
- Print remains the dominant surface, which favours a physical store: F002.
- Communal reading is almost untapped: F004.

## G2 — Reading time versus screen time in a real day

Time-use measurement, not opinion, gives the campaign its hard contrast: the average day holds a few minutes of reading against hours of television (F005). The gap is widest exactly among the young adults an independent store most wants back (F006). This is the strongest single number in the deck: it is a *time* problem, not a *love of books* problem.

- The daily contrast: F005.
- The age split inside that contrast: F006.

## G3 — Is participation falling

Two independent instruments, one American and one British, both point down. The NEA's national arts-participation survey shows literary reading and book engagement slipping (F007). The UK's Annual Literacy Survey, on a very large sample of 8- to 18-year-olds, records the lowest reading enjoyment in twenty years and a daily-reading figure below one in five (F008, F009). A material nuance to state honestly on the page: F007's decline softens when audiobook listening is counted, so the fall is partly a shift of surface, not only a loss of readers.

- US decline, with the audiobook caveat: F007.
- UK enjoyment at a 20-year low: F008.
- UK daily reading: F009.

## G4 — What happens at night

Night is where the two behaviours collide. Screen use in bed is now the normal case, not the exception (F011), and the dose-response evidence attaches a specific cost to each extra hour of it: measurably more insomnia and measurably less sleep (F010). This is the campaign's opening: the night is already a media slot, it is simply owned by something else.

- Prevalence of the competing behaviour: F011.
- Its measured cost: F010.

## G5 — Is reading in bed actually better

Yes, and on randomised evidence rather than folklore (F012). The effect is modest and self-reported, and the pitch must say so rather than overclaim: the trial measures whether people *felt* their sleep improved, and the difference is about fourteen percentage points.

- The trial and its size of effect: F012.

## G6 — The trade the fictional client sits in

The independent bookstore sector is not a decline story at the moment; the trade association's own count runs to several thousand member stores (F013). This lets the pitch position the fictional client as part of a living, growing trade rather than as a survivor.

- Trade scale: F013.

---

## Material conflicts and cautions

- **Audiobooks change the answer.** F007's decline is materially smaller once listening is counted, and F002 shows a real audiobook share. Any page claiming "reading is dying" would be unsupported; the defensible claim is that *attention at night* has moved, not that books lost their audience.
- **Self-report versus measurement.** F001–F004, F008–F009, F011 and F012 are self-reported; F005–F006 are time-diary measurements. The deck should lean the hard contrast on F005/F006 and treat the rest as supporting.
- **Different countries, different instruments.** F001–F007 and F010–F013 are US, UK and Norwegian respectively. Do not merge them into a single national claim.
- **F013 is a membership count, not a census** of every independent bookstore.
- **Sources that refused fetching during this research**: PubMed's abstract page returned only a cookie-consent wall; the Springer article page redirected to an identity-provider authorization URL; one press-release syndication host returned HTTP 429. In each case the claim was verified on an accessible mirror of the same primary record (PubMed Central for F012) or on the organisation's own site (F013).
