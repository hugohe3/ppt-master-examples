### Gap 1 -- Visible spectrum range

The visible spectrum is conventionally given as roughly 380 to 700 nanometers, with violet at the short-wavelength end and red at the long-wavelength end (F001). This is a widely repeated round number, not a hard physical wall: compiled textbook references show the cited cutoffs actually vary (380-700, 380-750, 400-700, or 380-780 nm depending on the source), which is why the boundary is best presented as conventionally soft rather than a sharp edge (F002). **Conflict to flag on the page**: don't present "380-700 nm" as an exact physical constant -- say the eye's sensitivity fades out gradually near both ends, and different authorities round the endpoints differently.

### Gap 2 -- Cone photoreceptors: three types and their peaks

Human color vision is trichromatic, built on three cone types conventionally named S (short-wavelength), M (medium-wavelength), and L (long-wavelength) for where their sensitivity peaks (F003). The classic microspectrophotometry measurement of human cone photopigments (Bowmaker & Dartnall, 1980) put the mean peak absorbance at approximately 420 nm for S cones, 534 nm for M cones, and 563 nm for L cones (F004). For scale, the retina holds roughly 6 million cones against roughly 120 million rods -- cones are only about 5% of all photoreceptors but dominate the fovea, where color and fine detail are resolved (F005).

### Gap 3 -- Tabulated cone sensitivity curve for a native chart

The authoritative tabulated dataset for this is the Stockman & Sharpe (2000) 2-degree cone fundamentals, maintained by the Colour & Vision Research Laboratory (CVRL) at the UCL Institute of Ophthalmology and downloadable at 0.1, 1, and 5 nm steps; these curves have been adopted by the CIE as its physiologically-relevant standard colorimetric functions (F006). **Unresolved**: this research pass could not retrieve the actual numeric (wavelength, S, M, L) rows from CVRL's site through the tools available -- repeated fetch attempts to cvrl.org were refused/rate-limited. ### Gap 4 -- Additive vs. subtractive mixing

Additive mixing combines colored light: it starts from black (no light) and adds red, green, and blue light, with all three at full intensity producing white; every screen -- monitor, phone, projector -- works this way (F007). Subtractive mixing combines physical pigments, inks, or dyes: it starts from white (fully reflected light), and each added cyan, magenta, or yellow pigment absorbs (subtracts) part of the spectrum, so adding more pigment makes the result darker; this is how all physical print works (F008). The two primary sets are complements of each other -- each additive primary is a secondary mixture in the subtractive system, and vice versa -- which is a clean way to connect the two halves of the workshop (F009).

### Gap 5 -- HSL and why lightness carries legibility

HSL represents a color as hue (an angle, 0-360 degrees around a color circle: red at 0/360, green at 120, blue at 240), saturation (a percentage, 100% fully saturated down to 0% gray), and lightness (a percentage, 0% black, 100% white, 50% a color's "normal" tone) (F010). The reason lightness/luminance -- not hue or saturation -- is the axis that actually carries legibility is structural: the W3C's own text-contrast rule (WCAG SC 1.4.3) is defined purely as a function of the two colors' relative luminance, with no hue or saturation term at all (F011), computed from gamma-linearized sRGB channels as L = 0.2126R + 0.7152G + 0.0722B (F012). That gives the workshop a clean demonstration: two colors can differ enormously in hue and still fail accessibility if their luminance is close, and two colors of the *same* hue can pass easily if their lightness differs enough.

### Gap 6 -- WCAG 2.x contrast thresholds

At Level AA, WCAG Success Criterion 1.4.3 requires a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1 for normal text (F013) and at least 3:1 for large text (F014). "Large text" is defined as at least 18 point, or at least 14 point bold, or an equivalent visual size for CJK fonts -- the spec's own conversion notes 14pt/18pt as roughly 18.5px/24px (F015). At Level AAA, SC 1.4.6 raises normal text to at least 7:1 and large text to at least 4.5:1 (F016, F017). A separate criterion, SC 1.4.11 (Non-text Contrast), sets a 3:1 minimum against adjacent colors for user-interface components and for graphical objects required to understand the content -- this is the rule that governs icons, chart lines, and form-field borders, not just body text (F018). The ratio itself is computed as (L1 + 0.05) / (L2 + 0.05) using the lighter and darker relative luminances, ranges from 1:1 to 21:1, and must not be rounded when checked against a threshold (F019).

### Gap 7 -- Color-vision-deficiency prevalence

The U.S. National Eye Institute states that about 1 in 12 men have a color vision deficiency, and that men carry a much higher risk than women (F020). Consistent with that, worldwide estimates put red-green color vision deficiency at about 8% of men and 0.5% of women (roughly 1 in 12 men, 1 in 200 women), rising to about 10-11% of men in some Scandinavian/Northern European populations (F021). Within that 8% of men, the type breakdown is roughly: 5% deuteranomalous (the single most common type), 1% deuteranopes, 1% protanomalous, and 1% protanopes (F022) -- a clean set of numbers for a native bar chart. Blue-yellow deficiency (tritanopia/tritanomaly) and total color blindness (achromatopsia) are both far rarer, on the order of 1 in 30,000-50,000 people (F023).

### Gap 8 -- Color harmony schemes as convention, not law

The complementary/analogous/triadic vocabulary most people learn traces to Johannes Itten's twelve-hue color wheel, published in his 1961 book *The Art of Color* and rooted in the color course he taught at the Bauhaus in the 1920s; Itten framed these as one of seven categories of *contrast* he catalogued for design teaching, not as a law of physics (F024). Albert Munsell built a competing system that divides hues into perceptually equal steps -- and because different color models yield different "complementary" pairs, the specific schemes taught in design are conventions of whichever model is chosen, not a single physical fact about light or pigment (F025). This is reinforced by the argument (Hard & Sivik, 2001) that because humans can perceptually distinguish on the order of millions of colors, any fixed predictive harmony formula is fundamentally unsound as a universal rule -- harmony judgments are contextual and perceptual (F026). This is the gap's core teaching point: harmony rules are useful heuristics inherited from specific historical teachers and systems, worth knowing, but not discoverable from physics the way the cone-sensitivity curves are.

### Gap 9 -- Why rainbow palettes are a bad default for ordered data

Borland & Taylor's widely cited 2007 paper argues the rainbow color map is rarely optimal for visualizing ordered/sequential data for three concrete reasons: it lacks perceptual ordering (equal-looking color steps don't correspond to equal data steps), it obscures data through uncontrolled luminance variation across hues, and it can actively mislead interpretation through gradients unrelated to the underlying data (F027). More recently, Crameri, Shephard & Heron (2020, *Nature Communications*) document that rainbow-like and red-green color maps remain common in published science despite visually distorting data and being unreadable to people with color-vision deficiency, and they propose perceptually uniform "scientific colour maps" as the fix (F028). Together these give the workshop both the classic indictment and a modern, CVD-linked follow-up -- a natural bridge back to Gap 7.

### Gap 10 -- A public-domain artwork for the palette-extraction demo

Katsushika Hokusai's *Under the Wave off Kanagawa* ("The Great Wave"), from the series *Thirty-six Views of Mount Fuji*, is a polychrome woodblock print made circa 1830-32 in Japan's Edo period, held in the collection of The Metropolitan Museum of Art (F029). It is a strong candidate for a live "extract a 5-color palette" exercise: the Met's Open Access program, launched February 7, 2017, releases images and data for public-domain works -- including this print -- under a CC0 designation, for unrestricted commercial and noncommercial reuse without permission or fee (F030). No documented dominant-color list was found on the museum's own record; the palette itself should be derived by the deck owner directly from the image, not invented here.

### Gap 11 -- Tools and further reading

For the closing page: WebAIM's Contrast Checker computes the WCAG contrast ratio between two colors and reports AA/AAA pass-fail for normal and large text (F031). The W3C's "How to Meet WCAG (Quick Reference)" is the filterable master list of every WCAG success criterion and technique (F032). Coblis lets a viewer upload an image and see a simulated rendering of it under each major type of color vision deficiency (F033). ColorBrewer helps choose sequential, diverging, and qualitative color schemes for maps and data visualizations -- directly relevant to the Gap 9 rainbow-palette lesson (F034). Adobe Color is a free web app for building and exploring color themes, including rule-based harmony generators tied back to the Gap 8 conventions (F035). And the CVRL database at the UCL Institute of Ophthalmology is where the Gap 3 cone-fundamentals data actually lives, for anyone who wants to go past the workshop into the primary physiological data (F036).
